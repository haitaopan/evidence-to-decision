## A Randomized Trial Without a Head-to-Head Comparison?

Imagine a Phase II clinical trial investigating two experimental treatments, A and B.

Patients are randomly assigned to receive one treatment or the other, with equal probability. Both groups follow the same general study procedures, and investigators collect information about treatment response, safety, and other clinical outcomes.

So far, this looks like an ordinary randomized trial.

But then we read the statistical analysis plan.

The primary efficacy analysis does not test whether A is better than B. Instead, each experimental treatment is evaluated separately against a historical control benchmark.

Treatment A is compared with historical outcomes.

Treatment B is compared with the same historical outcomes.

There is no planned primary hypothesis test directly comparing A with B.

This raises an obvious question:

**If the investigators are not primarily interested in comparing the two randomized groups, why randomize patients between them at all?**

Why not conduct two independent Phase II studies? Why not investigate A first and B afterward? Or simply enroll patients into two parallel cohorts without randomization?

At first glance, randomization appears to add complexity without contributing to the primary statistical analysis.

But the question becomes more interesting when we consider what Phase II trials are intended to accomplish.

A clinical trial is not necessarily designed to answer only one hypothesis-testing question. It may also help investigators learn about several promising strategies, evaluate their safety and feasibility, understand biological signals, and decide which treatments deserve further development.

Randomization can contribute to those objectives even when a conventional head-to-head efficacy test is not the primary goal.

The important qualification is that **the scientific benefits of randomization must be distinguished from the strength of evidence provided by historical comparisons.**

These are different matters, and confusing them can lead to misleading conclusions.

## A Hypothetical Rare-Disease Trial

Consider an entirely fictional rare pediatric disorder that causes progressive neurological impairment.

Investigators have developed two experimental treatment strategies. Both have encouraging biological rationales, but neither has sufficient clinical evidence to justify a definitive efficacy trial.

Because the disease is rare, recruiting enough patients for a large randomized study would be difficult.

Suppose a well-characterized historical cohort suggests that approximately 30% of patients receiving conventional care achieve clinical stabilization over six months.

Investigators propose a Phase II study enrolling 48 children, randomized equally between Treatments A and B.

The endpoint is clinical stabilization at six months, measured using a prespecified assessment and follow-up schedule.

For each experimental treatment, the investigators establish a separate statistical decision rule:

- A response rate consistent with the historical benchmark would be considered insufficiently promising.
- A meaningfully higher response rate would support further clinical development.

The analysis evaluates each experimental arm against the historical benchmark rather than formally testing A against B.

There are legitimate reasons to question this design. The historical benchmark may be unreliable, and 24 patients per arm may provide only limited information.

But assuming the investigators have carefully considered those limitations, randomization may still serve several purposes.

## 1. Randomization Makes the Two Experimental Groups More Comparable

Perhaps the most familiar property of randomization is its ability to reduce systematic differences between treatment groups.

Patients with rare diseases can differ substantially in characteristics that influence outcomes: age, disease severity, prior treatments, organ function, genetic abnormalities, and many other factors.

If investigators allocate treatments according to their preferences, even unintentionally, the resulting groups may differ in important ways.

For example, clinicians might preferentially assign patients with more advanced disease to Treatment B because they believe it has a stronger biological mechanism. Or they might assign healthier patients to Treatment A because it requires a more demanding procedure.

If outcomes subsequently differ, interpretation becomes difficult.

Did one treatment perform differently, or were the patients different to begin with?

Random assignment reduces the opportunity for systematic treatment selection based on these characteristics.

As emphasized in [ICH E9](https://www.fda.gov/regulatory-information/search-fda-guidance-documents/e9-statistical-principles-clinical-trials) and [ICH E10](https://www.fda.gov/regulatory-information/search-fda-guidance-documents/e10-choice-control-group-and-related-issues-clinical-trials), randomization provides a statistical foundation for comparing treatment groups and tends to balance measured and unmeasured prognostic factors.

However, the word *tends* matters.

**Randomization does not guarantee that two small Phase II groups will be perfectly balanced.**

With only 24 patients per arm, clinically important imbalances can occur by chance. Stratified randomization or other appropriate restricted allocation procedures may help balance a few particularly important characteristics, but they cannot eliminate every difference.

Nor does randomization prevent bias arising after treatment assignment.

Nevertheless, randomly allocating patients from a common eligible population generally provides a more defensible basis for interpreting differences between the two experimental groups than allowing clinicians to select treatments.

### But what if the trial is not testing A versus B?

That is where the distinction between primary and secondary scientific objectives becomes important.

Even without a powered A-versus-B efficacy comparison, investigators may want to understand:

- Whether one strategy produces more severe toxicities.
- Whether biological responses differ between strategies.
- Whether treatment preparation or administration is more feasible.
- Whether exploratory efficacy outcomes consistently favor one strategy.

Randomization helps make such observations more interpretable.

It does not turn an underpowered exploratory comparison into definitive evidence. But provided that post-randomization selection, missing data, and assessment differences are appropriately addressed, it can provide a stronger foundation for learning about differences between A and B.

Importantly, randomization does permit an internally randomized estimate of the A-versus-B contrast. The absence of a primary head-to-head hypothesis test does not make that contrast meaningless. It does, however, limit what can reasonably be concluded from an imprecise exploratory estimate.

## 2. Concurrent Enrollment Avoids Some Problems of Sequential Development

Now imagine a different approach.

Investigators study Treatment A first.

They enroll 24 patients, wait for follow-up, analyze the results, and then consider opening a separate study of Treatment B.

This sounds reasonable. It may even be operationally simpler.

But suppose the second study begins three years later.

During those three years, several things may change.

Clinical centers may improve their supportive care. New diagnostic methods may identify patients earlier. Referral patterns may shift. Eligibility criteria may evolve. Investigators may gain experience managing the disease.

Even if the two protocols look similar, the patients and clinical environment may not be comparable.

If Treatment B produces better outcomes, investigators may struggle to determine whether that reflects the treatment itself or changes in clinical practice.

Concurrent enrollment does not eliminate every source of variation, but it reduces the extent to which the two strategies are separated by calendar time.

When combined with concealed random allocation and consistent eligibility and follow-up procedures, it can also limit systematic differences in which patients receive each strategy during enrollment.

### There is also a clinical-development problem

Suppose Treatment A looks exceptionally promising.

Would investigators still be motivated to study Treatment B?

Perhaps not.

Researchers, funding agencies, and clinical collaborators might concentrate their resources on A. Recruitment to a future B study could become difficult, and the opportunity to investigate B might disappear.

Now consider the opposite situation.

Suppose A performs poorly. Enthusiasm for the entire therapeutic approach may decline, even if B has a different mechanism and could have been beneficial.

Studying both strategies concurrently may help preserve the opportunity to learn about each before the program commits to one direction.

This is a potentially important reason to design a randomized multiarm Phase II trial.

But it requires careful interpretation.

**Preserving multiple development options is a strategic advantage of concurrent investigation, not a statistical property guaranteed by randomization.**

Two nonrandomized cohorts could also enroll concurrently. Whether randomization adds sufficient value depends on the possibility of selection bias, practical allocation constraints, and the importance of interpreting differences between the experimental groups.

Likewise, concurrent enrollment is valuable only if the arms genuinely overlap in calendar time and receive reasonably consistent assessment and care.

Randomization cannot correct a study in which one arm is effectively recruited years after the other.

## 3. Early Randomization May Help Define the Treatment Strategy—But Time Zero Is a Separate Question

Some experimental treatments involve a long and complicated process.

After initial eligibility assessment, patients may undergo additional screening, preparatory therapy, treatment manufacturing, or other procedures before receiving the investigational intervention.

During this period, patients may deteriorate, become ineligible, or die.

This creates an important question:

**When should the outcome clock start?**

Suppose investigators identify 30 eligible patients for an experimental treatment, but only 24 eventually receive it.

The other six patients experience clinical deterioration or other problems before administration.

If investigators report outcomes only among the 24 patients who successfully received treatment, they may be evaluating a selected population with a better prognosis than the population initially considered for treatment.

For some endpoints, especially survival, this can introduce survivor selection or guarantee-time bias.

Patients must remain alive and sufficiently well to reach treatment administration before they can enter a treated-only analysis.

The resulting outcomes may appear more favorable, even if part of that apparent advantage reflects the exclusion of patients who experienced earlier events.

An earlier randomization point can help investigators define and study the entire assigned treatment strategy, including the period before intervention delivery.

For example, a trial might randomize patients when common eligibility criteria are satisfied and begin follow-up at that point.

Treatment failure, inability to receive the assigned therapy, subsequent treatment changes, and death would then need to be addressed according to the prespecified clinical question and endpoint definition.

However, an important distinction must be maintained.

**Randomization is not required to define time zero early.**

A nonrandomized prospective cohort can also establish eligibility and begin follow-up before treatment administration.

Conversely, an early-randomized trial can still introduce serious bias if investigators later exclude patients who fail to receive the intervention or redefine follow-up differently across treatment groups.

The correct approach depends on the *estimand*: the precise clinical quantity the study intends to estimate.

Are investigators interested in outcomes following assignment to a treatment strategy, including preparation and possible failure to receive treatment?

Or are they interested specifically in outcomes after treatment administration among patients who reach that point?

These are different questions.

[ICH E9(R1)](https://www.fda.gov/regulatory-information/search-fda-guidance-documents/e9r1-statistical-principles-clinical-trials-addendum-estimands-and-sensitivity-analysis-clinical) provides a framework for defining such questions and handling intercurrent events. The [target-trial literature](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jclinepi.2016.04.014) similarly emphasizes alignment between eligibility, treatment assignment, and the beginning of follow-up.

Early randomization can facilitate that alignment, but cannot replace careful endpoint and analysis design.

And when experimental arms are evaluated against historical controls, the historical patients require an appropriately aligned index time as well.

## 4. What Randomization Does Not Fix: The Historical Comparison

We now reach perhaps the most important limitation of this design.

Patients were randomized between Treatments A and B.

They were not randomized between Treatment A and historical standard care.

They were not randomized between Treatment B and historical standard care.

Consequently, randomization does not establish the comparability of either experimental group with the historical controls.

The distinction is straightforward but fundamental.

| Relationship | What the design supports |
| --- | --- |
| **A versus B** | Randomized comparability in expectation, supporting exploratory or appropriately planned direct comparisons |
| **A and B enrolled concurrently** | Reduced calendar-time separation and opportunities for consistent clinical procedures |
| **A versus historical controls** | A nonrandomized comparison dependent on historical evidence quality and comparability assumptions |
| **B versus historical controls** | The same nonrandomized limitations |

Imagine that conventional care has improved substantially since the historical data were collected.

The historical stabilization rate was 30%, but patients receiving contemporary conventional care might now achieve 45%.

If Treatments A and B produce stabilization rates of 50% and 48%, both might appear promising against the original historical benchmark.

Yet neither result necessarily demonstrates a meaningful improvement over contemporary care.

Randomization between A and B does nothing to resolve this discrepancy.

Other important differences may involve disease severity, eligibility, prior treatment, endpoint assessment, follow-up schedules, and the timing of treatment initiation.

[ICH E10](https://www.fda.gov/regulatory-information/search-fda-guidance-documents/e10-choice-control-group-and-related-issues-clinical-trials) has long emphasized the limitations of externally controlled trials. [FDA's February 2023 draft guidance on externally controlled studies](https://www.fda.gov/regulatory-information/search-fda-guidance-documents/considerations-design-and-conduct-externally-controlled-trials-drug-and-biological-products) likewise highlights population comparability, outcome assessment, and index-time alignment. The latter document remains draft, nonbinding guidance, not final guidance.

These concerns are not eliminated by using randomization elsewhere in the design.

An appropriate historical comparison therefore requires its own scientific justification.

Investigators should establish that the historical patients represent a clinically relevant comparison population, that endpoints are sufficiently compatible, and that treatment-era differences are understood.

The uncertainty in an estimated historical response rate also matters. As [Thall and Simon](https://doi.org/10.1002/sim.4780090304) discussed in 1990, treating historical outcomes as perfectly known can produce misleading statistical conclusions.

A one-sample test against a fixed benchmark may be appropriate for a defined Phase II screening decision, but its nominal error probabilities do not automatically protect against an incorrect or nontransportable benchmark.

Historical comparisons can be useful—particularly for preliminary screening when disease outcomes are well characterized—but their inferential limitations remain.

## 5. A Significant Result for A and a Nonsignificant Result for B Do Not Establish Superiority

Suppose the trial is completed.

Treatment A meets its prespecified efficacy criterion against the historical benchmark.

Treatment B does not.

Can investigators conclude that A is better than B?

No.

The two primary analyses addressed different hypotheses:

- Does Treatment A meet its efficacy criterion relative to the historical benchmark?
- Does Treatment B meet its efficacy criterion relative to the historical benchmark?

Neither directly tests whether A is superior to B.

A result that crosses a statistical threshold for one treatment while another narrowly misses it may arise because of sampling variability.

The distinction between a statistically significant result and a nonsignificant result is not itself evidence of a statistically significant difference—a general statistical principle explained by [Gelman and Stern](https://doi.org/10.1198/000313006X152649).

Investigators may examine the observed A-versus-B difference, with an appropriate estimate of uncertainty, because the treatments were randomized.

But if the study was not designed with sufficient precision to evaluate that contrast, the result may remain inconclusive.

This distinction matters especially in small Phase II studies, where a difference of one or two patient outcomes can substantially change observed response rates.

Another consideration arises when both experimental arms are separately tested against historical evidence.

If the overall development program regards success in *either* arm as a positive finding, the probability of advancing at least one ineffective treatment may be greater than the nominal false-positive probability assigned to an individual comparison.

The relevant multiplicity and decision-error properties depend on the prespecified rules and the statistical dependence between analyses, including any uncertainty shared through the historical benchmark.

A sound design should examine these operating characteristics before recruitment begins.

## 6. What If Both Arms, One Arm, or Neither Arm Succeeds?

The answer depends on what the investigators intend to do with the Phase II results.

If **both treatments meet their efficacy criteria**, both may deserve further investigation. The next decision could consider toxicity, feasibility, biological activity, treatment burden, cost, and the degree of uncertainty surrounding each result.

The trial has not automatically established which treatment is more effective.

If **only one treatment meets its criterion**, investigators may reasonably prioritize that strategy for additional study.

But this is a development decision, not proof that the selected treatment is superior to the other.

If **neither treatment meets its criterion**, investigators may decide not to advance either strategy under the current development plan.

That does not necessarily establish that both treatments lack biological activity or clinical benefit. The trial may have had limited sensitivity to certain benefits, the endpoint may have been unsuitable, or the original efficacy expectations may have been unrealistic.

These distinctions are consistent with the broader literature on Phase II screening and selection designs.

[Simon, Wittes, and Ellenberg](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/4075313/) discussed randomized selection approaches in which choosing a promising treatment for further study was explicitly distinguished from establishing definitive superiority.

Subsequent work by [Sargent and Goldberg](https://doi.org/10.1002/sim.704) and by [Rubinstein and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.1200/JCO.2005.01.149) further developed the methodological landscape of screening, selecting, and comparing treatments in Phase II development.

Importantly, a trial that separately evaluates two randomized experimental arms against historical benchmarks is not automatically a formal randomized selection design.

A selection design requires an explicit selection objective and decision rule. Screening criteria, treatment ranking, and formal comparative testing can be combined in different ways, but they should not be confused.

The question is not merely which arm generated the smallest p-value.

It is which clinical-development decision the available evidence can reasonably support.

## 7. Could Another Design Be Better?

Randomization between two experimental treatments is not always necessary.

Consider five possible designs.

| Design | Principal advantage | Main limitation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Two sequential Phase II cohorts** | May be simpler and allow learning from A before studying B | Vulnerable to calendar-time changes and shifting development priorities |
| **Two concurrent nonrandomized cohorts** | Allows simultaneous investigation without random allocation | Clinical or investigator selection can produce systematic differences |
| **Two randomized experimental cohorts, each assessed historically** | Supports balanced concurrent learning and exploratory A-versus-B interpretation | Neither historical comparison becomes randomized |
| **Randomized A-versus-B comparison** | Directly estimates comparative treatment performance | May require greater sample size and does not independently establish superiority to standard care |
| **Randomized trial with concurrent standard-care control** | Provides a stronger basis for estimating effects relative to current care | May require additional patients and raise feasibility or ethical challenges |

There is no universally best design.

If the central question is whether either experimental treatment demonstrates sufficient preliminary activity to justify further research, historical-benchmark screening may be useful.

If the question is which of two treatments produces better outcomes, a prespecified randomized A-versus-B comparison is more directly aligned with that objective.

If the question is whether an experimental treatment improves outcomes relative to contemporary standard care, a randomized concurrent standard-care group generally provides more compelling evidence.

And if randomization is impractical, carefully designed concurrent nonrandomized cohorts may still be informative, provided their limitations are acknowledged.

The choice depends on what must be learned, how many patients are available, the reliability of existing evidence, and the consequences of making an incorrect decision.

## 8. Trial Design Is Also About Managing Uncertainty and Development Decisions

It is tempting to view clinical trial design primarily through the lens of hypothesis testing.

We specify a null hypothesis, calculate a sample size, collect data, and determine whether a p-value crosses a threshold.

These steps are important, but they describe only part of clinical development.

Particularly in rare diseases, the available patients and research resources may be too limited to pursue every promising treatment through a definitive trial.

Phase II studies therefore play a broader role.

They help determine which interventions merit further investigation, which safety concerns require attention, whether complex strategies are feasible, and what uncertainty remains before larger commitments are justified.

A randomized Phase II design may support these decisions without including a conventional primary A-versus-B superiority test.

But its value depends on what randomization is actually accomplishing.

Randomization may make the experimental groups more comparable. Concurrent enrollment may reduce temporal differences. Early assignment may help define the treatment strategies being evaluated.

None of these benefits makes a historical comparison internally randomized.

Likewise, a treatment meeting a Phase II efficacy threshold may justify further development without establishing comparative superiority or definitive clinical benefit.

The design succeeds scientifically when its conclusions remain within the boundaries of the evidence it generates.

## Conclusion: Randomization Is a Tool, Not a Declaration of Intent

A randomized trial without a primary head-to-head comparison is not necessarily contradictory.

It may be designed to investigate multiple experimental strategies simultaneously, reduce systematic allocation differences, improve interpretation of exploratory outcomes, and inform decisions about future clinical development.

These can be legitimate reasons to randomize.

But randomization should never be treated as a general certificate of validity.

It protects comparisons through the particular allocation process in which it is used. It does not extend that protection to external patients who were never part of the randomization.

Nor does the presence of randomization tell us what scientific claim the trial was designed to establish.

The more useful question is therefore not simply, *Why did the investigators randomize?*

It is:

**What uncertainty was randomization intended to reduce, and what uncertainty remains when the trial is complete?**

That distinction is central to designing trials that do more than produce statistically significant results.

It is how clinical trials generate evidence that can responsibly guide the next decision.
