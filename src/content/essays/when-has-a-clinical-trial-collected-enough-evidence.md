*Beyond Early Stopping: From Completing a Trial to Completing Its Scientific Mission*

## A Search That Ended Before the Supplies Ran Out

Imagine organizing a search for someone lost in the wilderness.

Before setting out, the rescue team carefully estimates the resources required. They prepare enough food, water, equipment, and personnel to sustain a complete search.

Suppose the supplies could support ten days of searching.

On the second day, the team finds the missing person.

Would anyone be disappointed that the search ended eight days ahead of schedule?

Would the team insist on continuing for another eight days simply because those resources had already been allocated?

Of course not.

They would celebrate. The search had accomplished its purpose.

This compelling analogy comes from a November 2026 LinkedIn reflection by Kert Viele, Director of Research at Berry Consultants, who questioned our habitual use of the term *stopping a trial early*.

His original words deserve to be read in full:

> Suppose we are conducting a search for someone lost in the wilderness. We pack enough supplies so we can do a complete search and head out. If we find the person before we run out of supplies and head back, we don’t say “we stopped the search early,” We say “we found them” and celebrate.

The observation is deceptively simple.

**The purpose of a search is not to exhaust its supplies. It is to find the person.**

Why, then, do we so often describe a clinical trial that reaches a compelling conclusion before its planned maximum sample size as having stopped early?

Perhaps the issue is not merely our terminology. Perhaps it reflects something deeper about how we think about clinical trial design.

We sometimes confuse completing the plan with accomplishing the scientific mission.

And those are not necessarily the same thing.

## The Planned Sample Size Is Not the Scientific Destination

Consider a randomized clinical trial designed to enroll 200 patients.

The number is typically justified by assumptions about the treatment effect, variability, type I error, statistical power, and other design characteristics.

But there is nothing scientifically magical about patient number 200.

We do not believe that the question remains unanswerable after patient 199 and suddenly becomes answerable after patient 200.

Rather, sample size planning is a way of managing uncertainty before the data exist.

Once a trial begins, the information we observe is random. Outcomes vary, events occur at different times, and treatment effects may appear stronger or weaker than anticipated.

An appropriately designed sequential trial recognizes this uncertainty by allowing decisions at prespecified interim analyses.

In such a design, the final sample size may itself be random.

The planned maximum sample size establishes the resources potentially required. It need not be a number that every trial must reach.

There is, however, an important statistical distinction.

A conventional fixed-sample trial designed with 80% power does not have an 80% probability of reaching a conclusion before the planned sample size. Statistical power refers to the probability of meeting the specified statistical success criterion under particular assumptions about the true treatment effect.

The probability of stopping at an interim analysis requires a sequential design and an evaluation of its operating characteristics.

Thus, the lesson is not that sample size planning is unnecessary.

It is that sample size planning serves a broader scientific objective.

**The number of patients we planned to study is not, by itself, the question we set out to answer.**

## What Does It Mean to Have Found the Answer?

The search-and-rescue analogy becomes even more interesting when we examine its limits.

In a search, finding the missing person is usually a clearly recognizable accomplishment.

In a clinical trial, what exactly constitutes finding the answer?

I find it helpful to extend the original analogy into three different situations.

### Scenario One: The Missing Person Has Been Found

The rescue team locates the missing person and confirms their identity.

The immediate search objective has been achieved.

Continuing to search the surrounding forest would not advance that objective.

A clinical parallel would be a randomized trial in which the accumulating evidence crosses a prespecified efficacy stopping boundary.

Provided the design and analysis appropriately control statistical errors and support the intended inference, the trial may have reached the threshold required to stop further randomization.

Stopping at that point is not necessarily a deviation from the scientific plan.

It may be exactly what the plan was designed to permit.

The crucial distinction is that the stopping rule was established and evaluated before the observed results were known.

The trial has not merely produced an exciting result. It has met a defined evidentiary standard.

### Scenario Two: The Team Finds a Promising Clue

Suppose the rescue team discovers a piece of clothing or fresh footprints.

The discovery is encouraging.

It may substantially improve the team's understanding of where to search next.

But it does not establish that the missing person has been found.

Clinical trials face an analogous problem.

An interim analysis may reveal a surprisingly large treatment effect or a nominally small P value.

These findings can be encouraging without necessarily providing sufficient evidence for a confirmatory conclusion.

If the result has not met the trial's prespecified stopping criteria, calling the mission complete would be premature.

There is another important complication.

Trials stopped following unusually favorable interim results may overestimate the underlying treatment effect.

This is not simply a theoretical concern. Systematic investigations of randomized trials stopped for apparent benefit have documented the potential for exaggerated effect estimates, particularly when relatively few outcome events have occurred.

The distinction matters.

Evidence that a treatment has an effect is not identical to precise evidence about the magnitude of that effect.

A treatment may truly be beneficial even when the observed estimate is overly optimistic.

**Finding a promising clue is not the same as finding the person. Demonstrating an effect is not the same as fully characterizing its magnitude.**

### Scenario Three: The Person Has Been Found, but the Rescue Is Not Over

Now imagine that the rescue team finds the missing person injured and severely dehydrated.

The search is over.

But the rescue operation is not.

The person still needs medical assessment, stabilization, and transportation to safety.

This is perhaps the most important extension of the analogy for clinical research.

A trial may cross its prespecified efficacy boundary and appropriately stop further randomization.

Yet substantial clinical uncertainties may remain.

How large is the treatment benefit?

How durable is the response?

What are the consequences of prolonged treatment?

Are there late toxicities?

Will the apparent benefit translate into better long-term survival or quality of life?

In pediatric oncology, these questions can be especially consequential.

A convincing early efficacy signal may support an important clinical development decision while still leaving substantial uncertainty about long-term benefit and harm.

Stopping enrollment does not automatically mean stopping treatment, ending follow-up, or terminating every component of evidence generation.

**A statistical conclusion can be reached before the broader clinical evidence package is complete.**

Recognizing that distinction allows us to stop doing what is no longer necessary while continuing what remains scientifically and ethically important.

## Enough Evidence—for What?

The original search analogy leads to a more fundamental question.

When we ask whether a clinical trial has collected enough data, what do we really mean?

Enough data to reject a null hypothesis?

Enough to estimate a treatment effect precisely?

Enough to decide whether a larger trial is justified?

Enough to support regulatory approval?

Enough to change clinical practice?

These are different questions, and they may require different amounts and types of evidence.

It is useful to distinguish four dimensions of evidentiary adequacy.

**Statistical sufficiency** concerns whether the evidence meets the prespecified inferential requirements, including appropriate control of statistical errors.

**Estimation adequacy** concerns the reliability and precision of the estimated treatment effect, including the potential impact of sequential monitoring and stopping.

**Clinical evidence adequacy** concerns whether the available information adequately characterizes benefits, risks, durability, and relevance to the intended patient population.

**Decision readiness** concerns whether the accumulated evidence is sufficient to justify a particular action.

These dimensions overlap, but none automatically guarantees the others.

A trial might reach a statistically valid conclusion about efficacy while providing relatively limited information about late adverse events.

A small exploratory trial might generate enough evidence to justify further development without establishing the evidence required for routine clinical use.

Conversely, a trial may have little prospect of achieving its confirmatory objective and therefore warrant discontinuation, even though substantial uncertainty about the treatment remains.

This is closely aligned with the logic of the ICH E9(R1) estimand framework: the scientific question and the treatment effect of interest should be defined before choosing how to generate and analyze the evidence.

The question should come before the calculation.

And the decision should help determine what evidence is needed.

**Evidence is sufficient only relative to the question being asked and the decision it is expected to support.**

This may be the most useful way to extend Viele's original insight.

## Stopping for Futility Is a Different Kind of Decision

Let us return once more to the forest.

Suppose the rescue team searches for several days without finding the missing person.

Weather conditions deteriorate. The available clues become less informative. Continuing the same search strategy appears increasingly unlikely to succeed.

The team may reasonably decide to suspend the search or redirect resources toward a different approach.

But it would be inappropriate to celebrate as though the missing person had been found.

The same distinction is essential in clinical trials.

Stopping for efficacy and stopping for futility may both be justified, but they do not mean the same thing.

An efficacy stopping decision may indicate that the available evidence meets a prespecified standard for concluding that treatment is beneficial.

A futility stopping decision usually reflects a judgment that continuing the trial is unlikely to achieve its intended objective.

For example, conditional power estimates the probability of satisfying a specified final success criterion, conditional on the data observed so far and assumptions about future outcomes.

A low conditional power may support discontinuing enrollment.

It does not, by itself, demonstrate that the treatment has no meaningful biological or clinical effect.

Nor is failure to demonstrate superiority equivalent to establishing equivalence or the absence of benefit.

This distinction is particularly important in rare diseases and early pediatric oncology development, where small samples, heterogeneous populations, and uncertain treatment effects are common.

Sometimes discontinuing a trial is scientifically responsible precisely because additional data are unlikely to justify the burden and resources required.

The trial may not have resolved the original scientific uncertainty.

It may nevertheless have reached a defensible decision.

**A trial can reach a justified stopping decision without resolving every scientific uncertainty.**

That is a different kind of success—not necessarily success for the treatment, but potentially success in the responsible conduct of research.

## Why Statistical Discipline Still Matters

The argument that trials should stop when sufficient information is available might seem to invite frequent examination of the data and immediate stopping whenever a favorable result appears.

That would be a dangerous interpretation.

If investigators repeatedly test accumulating data and use an unadjusted conventional significance threshold at every opportunity, the probability of falsely declaring efficacy can increase.

Group sequential methods address this problem through appropriately calibrated stopping boundaries and monitoring plans.

Bayesian adaptive designs provide other ways to formulate decisions using posterior or predictive probabilities, with relevant operating characteristics evaluated according to the trial's scientific and regulatory objectives.

In either framework, flexibility does not eliminate the need for discipline.

The stopping rules, analysis methods, information requirements, and safeguards for trial integrity should be established and justified in advance.

The FDA's 2019 guidance on adaptive clinical trials emphasizes error control, reliable treatment effect estimation, prospective planning, and maintenance of trial integrity.

These principles do not exist to prevent a trial from ending when sufficient evidence is available.

They make such decisions credible.

The goal is not to stop whenever an interim result looks attractive.

**The goal is to design a trial in which a decision to stop can be scientifically defended when the relevant information has accumulated.**

There is also an important distinction between the timing of a stopping decision and the reliability of the resulting treatment effect estimate.

An efficacy boundary may provide a valid basis for hypothesis testing without ensuring that a conventional unadjusted estimate is unbiased or sufficiently precise.

A successful stopping rule and a satisfactory estimation strategy are related, but separate, design responsibilities.

## Continuing the Trial Is Not Free

There is an ethical dimension to this discussion that deserves equal attention.

Suppose a trial has accumulated highly persuasive evidence that one treatment is superior.

Continuing randomization solely to reach an originally planned sample size may impose avoidable burdens on patients.

But stopping too soon also has consequences.

Insufficient information about safety, durability, or effect magnitude may impair decisions affecting many future patients.

The important question, then, is not whether collecting more data is inherently good or bad.

It is what additional decision-relevant information those data are expected to provide—and at what cost.

This does not mean that every trial requires a formal value-of-information analysis.

It means that the value of continued enrollment should be considered in relation to the remaining uncertainties, the decisions those uncertainties affect, and the ethical and operational burden of resolving them.

If the main efficacy question has been adequately answered, additional randomization may no longer be necessary.

If long-term safety remains uncertain, continued follow-up may be essential.

If a treatment has shown sufficient promise for further development but not enough evidence for clinical adoption, the next study should be designed around the unanswered questions.

The most efficient clinical development program is not necessarily the one that stops every trial as soon as possible.

It is the one that generates the evidence needed for important decisions without collecting information merely because it was originally planned.

## Returning to the Forest

I keep returning to Kert Viele's search-and-rescue story because of its simplicity.

The team prepares enough resources to conduct a complete search.

The missing person is found before those resources are exhausted.

No one laments that the team failed to use everything it had prepared.

The mission was accomplished.

But extending the analogy reveals something equally important.

Finding a clue is not finding the person.

Finding the person does not necessarily complete the rescue.

And deciding that a particular search strategy should stop does not mean the person has been found.

Clinical trials have the same distinctions.

A promising signal is not necessarily a confirmatory result.

A statistically successful primary endpoint does not necessarily complete the clinical evidence package.

And a justified futility decision does not necessarily establish the absence of treatment benefit.

I share Viele's concern that the phrase *early stopping* can unintentionally imply that reaching the planned maximum sample size is the natural definition of completing a trial.

The terminology remains useful in statistical and regulatory practice. But we should be careful about the conceptual assumptions we attach to it.

What matters is not whether the trial stops before or after an arbitrarily imagined ideal time.

What matters is whether the stopping decision is appropriate for the evidence, the scientific question, and the decision to be made.

**The purpose of a search is not to exhaust its supplies.**

**The purpose of a clinical trial is not to exhaust its sample size.**

Both are undertaken to accomplish a mission.

The harder question in clinical research is defining that mission precisely enough to know when it has been accomplished.

Perhaps that is where the deepest value of statistical science lies—not merely in calculating how much information we might need, but in helping determine when the information we have is sufficient for the decision that matters.
