An early oncology trial shows encouraging results. The treatment appears active, the safety profile looks manageable, and the research team is excited about what comes next.

But what exactly should come next?

Should we enroll more patients? Start a randomized trial? Move the treatment into an earlier line of therapy?

These are different decisions. And they should not all be justified by the same label: "promising."

This is a problem I have been thinking about in early-phase oncology development, particularly in pediatric and rare cancers. We spend considerable effort deciding whether a treatment shows enough activity to warrant further investigation. Yet the more consequential question is often left less clearly defined: **What does the evidence actually justify doing next?**

## One Signal, Several Possible Futures

Consider a treatment that shows encouraging responses in patients with relapsed or refractory cancer.

One reasonable next step might be to expand the study in the same patient population. This would allow investigators to gain more experience with efficacy, safety, and response durability.

Another option might be a randomized Phase II trial. Now the question is different. We need to consider a relevant comparator, a clinically meaningful treatment effect, and whether the available evidence is sufficient to justify comparative evaluation.

A third option might be to move the treatment into frontline therapy.

That is a much bigger change than simply enrolling more patients. Frontline patients may have different disease characteristics, expected outcomes, available treatments, and tolerance for toxicity. An effect observed in heavily pretreated patients may not carry over in the same way.

The same early trial can motivate all three discussions. But it does not necessarily provide equal support for all three studies.

**Evidence is not sufficient or insufficient in the abstract. It is sufficient for a particular decision—or it is not.**

## Moving Beyond a Single Go/No-Go Decision

Traditional early-phase oncology trials often lead to a go/no-go conclusion. A treatment is considered promising enough to continue, or it is not.

That distinction is useful. But it can also compress several different development questions into one answer.

In my recent work, I proposed an *Evidence-to-Decision (E2D)* framework to organize these questions more explicitly.

The starting point is straightforward: before calculating whether the evidence is strong enough, define the next study we are actually considering.

For each possible development action, we ask:

- What would the next study look like?
- What result would count as success?
- What evidence would we want before taking that step?
- What evidence do we already have?
- What assumptions are we making about how today's results will apply to the future study?

Only then do we ask how strongly the existing evidence supports that particular next step.

Statistical methods, including predictive probabilities, can help quantify this support. But the probability belongs to a specified future study under specified assumptions. It is not a general score of how promising the treatment is.

This distinction changes how we interpret early evidence.

## What Happens When Different Decisions Are Examined Separately?

In the E2D paper, I explored this idea using simulated early-phase oncology trials motivated by rare pediatric cancer development.

The results illustrated several clinically meaningful situations.

With intermediate efficacy and acceptable toxicity, some simulated trials provided support for additional enrollment or randomized evaluation in the existing treatment setting, without yet supporting a move into frontline therapy.

With stronger efficacy and reassuring safety, multiple development paths could become supportable at the same time.

And when toxicity concerns increased, even strong treatment activity did not necessarily justify the more consequential development options.

These are not universal rules for oncology development. The simulations demonstrate what becomes visible when evidence is evaluated against specific future studies rather than summarized by one overall judgment.

An encouraging result does not automatically justify every possible next step. And several next steps may be scientifically defensible without one being the obvious winner.

## Sometimes the Best Next Step Is to Learn Something Specific

There is another consequence of thinking this way.

When the evidence does not support an important next study, the natural reaction is often to ask for more data.

But more data about what?

If the concern is limited safety experience, additional safety follow-up may matter more than another early efficacy assessment.

If the concern is whether the treatment will work in a different patient population, simply expanding the original cohort may not resolve that uncertainty.

If the concern is an uncertain control outcome, better comparative or historical evidence may be more useful than enrolling additional patients on the experimental treatment alone.

The point is not that we always need more evidence. It is that we should identify **which missing evidence could actually change the decision.**

This is particularly important in rare cancers, where patients are limited and every additional study carries an opportunity cost.

A decision to wait, expand, randomize, or change direction should ideally follow from the uncertainty we are trying to resolve—not simply from a habit of collecting more observations.

## Statistics Should Clarify the Decision, Not Make It for Us

There is an important distinction between identifying which studies the evidence can support and deciding which study to pursue.

Even when several options are supported, investigators still need to consider clinical relevance, patient availability, feasibility, competing trials, resources, and patient and family priorities.

A statistical model cannot settle all of those questions. Nor should it.

Its contribution is to make the evidentiary reasoning clearer: what we know, what we assume, which actions the evidence supports, and where uncertainty still matters.

E2D is an attempt to make that reasoning more explicit and reproducible. The current paper provides a conceptual framework and a calibrated simulation demonstration. Applying it to real development decisions will require close collaboration with clinical investigators and careful examination of the assumptions relevant to each setting.

The ultimate aim is not to replace judgment with an algorithm. It is to make clinical and statistical judgment work together more effectively.

## Enough Evidence—for What?

Early oncology development will always involve uncertainty. We cannot eliminate it before deciding what to do next.

But we can be much clearer about the decision we are making and the evidence needed to justify it.

A promising signal may be the beginning of an important development program.

**It is not, by itself, a development plan.**

Before asking whether we have enough evidence to move forward, perhaps the better question is:

**Enough evidence for what?**
